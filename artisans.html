Craft that still lives in the villages that made it.

Across West Bengal, entire districts still carry a craft passed down through generations: a family's Patachitra scroll, a village's Dokra cast, a household's Kantha stitch. This is where we introduce those artisans, their culture, and the work you can bring home.

Why this page exists

Adaaye began with one folk artist and her brush. But Bengal's craft traditions run far wider than one studio: Dokra metal-casters in Bankura, Chhau mask-makers in Purulia, Baluchari weavers in Murshidabad, Patachitra painters in the villages around Pingla. Each district has its own material, its own myth, its own rhythm of work.

This page is our way of pointing you toward that wider map: the district, the craft, the people behind it, and the pieces you can order directly to support them. It's a growing page. As we visit more villages and confirm more artisan partners, we'll add their names, faces and workshops here.

Paschim Medinipur

Naya village, Pingla

Patachitra, scroll painting and song

Culture and story

Naya is a village where nearly every household paints. Patachitra artists, known as patuas, work in long vertical scrolls, telling stories from myth, history and current events panel by panel, and singing the story aloud (the pater gaan) as they unroll it. Natural pigments are still ground from turmeric, lampblack, tamarind seed and stone.

Lifestyle and passion

Patua families paint through the day in open courtyards, often with three generations working the same scroll: an elder mixing pigment, a parent outlining figures, a child filling colour. The craft is inseparable from performance. Patuas remain travelling storytellers as much as painters, carrying scrolls from village to village during festival season.

Bankura

Bikna and Panchmura villages

Dokra metal-casting and Bankura terracotta

Culture and story

Bikna's Dokra artisans use the 4,000-year-old lost-wax casting method: a clay core wrapped in wax thread, coated in clay again, then fired so the wax melts away and molten brass fills the void. Nearby Panchmura is home to the terracotta horse, the elongated clay figure that has become a symbol of Bengal's folk art on the national stage.

Lifestyle and passion

Both crafts are worked in home courtyards using wood-fired kilns built by the families themselves. Dokra casters often say every piece is unique because the mould is destroyed to release it, so no two casts are ever identical. Terracotta potters describe the horse as something they can shape with their eyes closed, taught hand-over-hand since childhood.

Purulia

Charida village

Chhau mask-making

Culture and story

Charida is known as Bengal's "mask village". Almost every home makes the papier-mâché and clay masks used in Purulia Chhau, a masked martial dance-drama performed at Chaitra Sankranti depicting scenes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata. Each mask is built in layers over a clay mould, painted, and finished with foil, beads and feather ornaments.

Lifestyle and passion

Mask-making is seasonal and communal. Families work through the winter ahead of the Chhau festival season, drying layers of paper in the sun and finishing headdresses late into the night before a performance. Many mask-makers are also Chhau dancers or drummers themselves, so the mask is made by someone who knows exactly how it needs to move.

Birbhum

Villages around Santiniketan

Kantha embroidery

Culture and story

Kantha began as a way to give old, layered cotton saris and dhotis a second life, quilted together with running stitch into blankets, wraps and covers. In Birbhum, that running stitch has grown into fine figurative embroidery: birds, boats, trees of life and courtyard scenes worked entirely by hand, thread pulled from the borders of old saris.

Lifestyle and passion

Kantha is largely stitched by women, often in groups, with verandah conversations timed to the rhythm of the needle. A single large kantha can take weeks to months of evening work between household duties, which is part of why makers describe it less as a craft and more as a diary stitched in thread.

Murshidabad

Bishnupur weaving lineage, Murshidabad looms

Baluchari silk weaving

Culture and story

Baluchari sarees are woven on jacquard looms with pallus depicting entire mythological or courtly scenes (chariots, musicians, dancers) worked directly into the silk without embroidery. The craft moved to the Bishnupur–Murshidabad weaving belt generations ago and has stayed a family trade, with pattern cards (the loom's "memory") often passed down like heirlooms.

Lifestyle and passion

A single Baluchari saree can take a week or more on the loom, with weavers working in small home workshops, often two people to a loom for the heavier jacquard pulls. Weavers speak of the pallu scene as the part they take the most pride in. It's the part a saree is remembered for.

Nadia

Krishnanagar

Clay doll-making

Culture and story

Krishnanagar's clay artists are known for hyper-realistic figures (fruit-sellers, farmers, freedom fighters, festival deities) modelled in unglazed and painted clay with a level of detail once commissioned by royal courts. The tradition dates back over 200 years and remains centred on a handful of artisan lanes in the town.

Lifestyle and passion

Each figure is built in stages (armature, rough clay, fine detailing, sun-drying, painting), often across several family members working different stages of the same workshop. Artisans describe the goal as making clay "breathe": a wrinkle, a fold of cloth, an expression caught mid-sentence.

South 24 Parganas

Sundarbans-fringe villages

Shola pith craft

Culture and story

Shola is a soft, milky-white wetland reed that malakars (garland-makers) carve into astonishingly fine ornamental work: bridal topors and mukuts, Durga Puja idol decorations, and delicate flower and bird models. The material is light enough to float and carved with nothing more than a thin blade.

Lifestyle and passion

Work is highly seasonal, peaking hard in the weeks before Durga Puja and the wedding season, when malakar households work near round-the-clock on temple and pandal ornamentation. Outside season, the same fine-blade skill turns to smaller decorative pieces and export work.

Cooch Behar

North Bengal wetland belts

Sitalpati (cane mat) weaving

Culture and story

Sitalpati, literally "cool mat", is woven from thin strips of murta cane, split and polished until pliable enough to weave as fine as cloth. Traditionally used as bedding and floor mats through Bengal's humid summers, the weave is now shaped into bags, coasters and modern home pieces.

Lifestyle and passion

Weavers first harvest and process the cane themselves, splitting, boiling, sun-drying and shaving each strip by hand before a single row is woven. It's slow, seasonal, river-adjacent work, and weavers describe a good mat as one that stays cool to the touch no matter the season.
